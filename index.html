01About

How I ended up here

Albanian is my first language, my passport is Greek, I study in Dutch-speaking Limburg in English, and I spent a semester in Rome. None of that was a plan. It does mean that by the time I met the phrase "cross-country heterogeneity" in a lecture, it already described something I had lived rather than something I had to imagine.

One central bank sets one interest rate for twenty countries. I find it genuinely strange that this works at all, and I want to know where it works less well.

That is what my thesis turned out to be about. The euro area sets a single policy rate for economies where cash settles anywhere between a fifth and two thirds of everyday payments, and since the machinery that carries a rate change runs through banks, and cash does not, I wanted to see whether the difference shows up in the data. It does, faintly, at the horizons where theory says it should.

The part of the work I like least is the estimation. The part I like most is the hour afterwards, when you have to explain what you found to someone who has no reason to care about your standard errors and every reason to care about the answer.

Before Maastricht I did not expect to end up in economics at all. I came to it through wanting to understand institutions rather than markets, which is probably why the policy side keeps pulling me harder than the finance side.

Outside the degree I chaired my study association's anniversary committee, which meant a year of budgets, venues and several hundred emails, and taught me that most of what looks like a good event on the night was decided months earlier in a spreadsheet. 02Research

Does monetary policy work less well where people pay in cash?

Monetary Policy Transmission in the Euro Area: Low-Cash versus High-Cash Economies

BSc thesis· June 2026· Supervisor: Enrico Wegner· Graded 9.0 / 10

The ECB sets one policy rate for an area where cash settles 22 per cent of point-of-sale transactions in the Netherlands and 62 per cent in Austria. Because the standard channels of monetary transmission run through the banking system, and cash payments bypass it, the question is whether policy transmits more weakly where cash is more prevalent.

I estimate country-level structural VARs for thirteen euro-area economies using high-frequency policy surprises, then pool the impulse responses in a cross-country regression that interacts each horizon with the country's cash share. Higher cash usage is associated with a weaker GDP response, concentrated at the horizons where output troughs and robust to dropping any single country. With thirteen countries the evidence is best read as suggestive rather than settled, and the price-side results are weaker still.

The finding bears on a live policy question. By drawing cash transactions into the banking system, a digital euro could strengthen transmission, and the size of that gain depends on design choices currently under debate.

The main result. The effect of a one-point higher cash share on the output response to an ECB surprise, by quarter, with 90 per cent bands. It rises into the trough and fades after quarter six, which is the shape the mechanism predicts.

In plain termsWhy would cash matter at all?

When the ECB changes its interest rate, the change travels to the economy mostly through banks. Loans get dearer, deposits pay differently, collateral is worth more or less. All of that needs your money to be sitting in, and moving through, a bank.

Cash does not move through a bank. If you are paid in cash and you spend it in cash, that money never touches the machinery the central bank is pulling on. So in a country where most payments are cash, you would expect the same interest rate decision to have a smaller effect. That is the whole idea, and the point of the thesis is to check whether it shows up in the data.

If you did econThe mechanism and the test

Of the five channels in the standard transmission taxonomy, three are bank-intermediated: bank lending, balance sheet, and deposits. Each depends on the deposit base and on activity being routed through bank balance sheets, both of which a high cash share erodes. The interest-rate and asset-price channels are affected only indirectly. So the prediction is not that transmission stops, but that the bank-funding-dependent channels weaken first and most, and that the effect should show up at the horizons where those channels operate rather than uniformly.

The test needs cross-country variation in cash use plus a shock that is common to everyone, which is what the euro area provides: one policy rate, and cash shares running from 22 to 62 per cent of point-of-sale transactions.

TechnicalSpecification and inference

Monetary surprises come from high-frequency movements in market rates around ECB announcements, aggregated to quarterly frequency. A structural VAR is estimated per country in log levels with the ECB non-energy commodity index and three lags; thirteen of nineteen members yield stable estimates on a sufficient sample.

The impulse responses are then pooled: y(c,h) = Σ D(h)·θ(h) + Σ (D(h)×cash(c))·γ(h) + ε, where the unit of observation is a country–horizon pair and the coefficients of interest are the interactions γ(h). Standard errors are clustered by country, with 90 per cent bands from a t-distribution on G−1 degrees of freedom.

Constraining γ to a single coefficient across all horizons gives 0.00042 (p = 0.56); averaging over twenty-one horizons dilutes a concentrated effect. Allowing it to vary by horizon puts it at quarters three to five, the pre-specified trough of the average output response, with a joint test over that window giving 0.00115 (t = 1.50, p = 0.159). Adding manufacturing intensity, a theory-motivated control for interest-sensitive durable-goods production, returns p-values of 0.018, 0.009 and 0.023 at the same horizons. The result is robust to leave-one-country-out.

03Policy writing

Turning results into something a decision-maker can use

Two short briefs drawn from the thesis: one on what the finding means for the digital euro, one on the country that behaved the wrong way.

Policy brief

The digital euro has a transmission dividend, and nobody is pricing it

The debate has settled on privacy, financial stability and holding limits. The transmission question is absent, and it runs opposite to the disintermediation worry: an instrument that displaces part of cash circulation brings transactions currently outside the mechanism inside it. That gain is a design variable, not a fixed property.

Policy brief

The country that moved the wrong way

Of thirteen economies, twelve contract after a contractionary surprise. Greece does not. The plausible explanations are not statistical accidents: they point at an impaired bank-lending channel, a condition under which standard transmission estimates mislead. Sign-check failures are findings, not noise.
